**Conclusion.** For the product as the owner defines it — an investment tool that makes per-company calls, shows their historical scorecard, and that others can use (tiers b4–b5) — letting others use it is **materially harder**, for three reasons that do not go away with copy edits: (1) it moves from advising oneself (unregulated) to advising others, where the boundary (compensation, holding out, impersonality) depends on facts the owner must keep true forever, and a state's rule may differ from the federal one; (2) the scorecard becomes a hypothetical-performance presentation to a retail audience, with a permanent presentation and position-disclosure discipline attached; (3) every personal-tier data licence surveyed excludes it, so the price data must be relicensed or the derived-number question settled in writing. ## Q2 — The per-company historical scorecard on a public page

**Is it a performance claim?** Yes, in substance. A per-company table of "outperform/underperform" calls with right/wrong and a hit rate is *hypothetical performance* in the SEC Marketing Rule's vocabulary (backtested and model results are named categories) and an efficacy claim under FTC §5, whatever unit it is expressed in; "the model was right 62% of the time on Alphabet" is received as "this tool predicts stock moves." The pre-registration's "descriptive, not inference" sentence is correct and must stay, but it does not change the reader's reception. The Marketing Rule does not formally apply to an unregistered individual; it is the best-articulated standard of what "not misleading" looks like for exactly this artifact, and the SEC staff's stated view is that hypothetical performance is very hard to present appropriately to a general retail website audience. Rule 10b-5 and FTC §5 apply regardless.

**May it appear on a public page?** Not today, and not as a standalone artifact. It may appear only after §4-1, §4-3, §4-4 and §4-10 are confirmed, the data-vendor position for derived numbers is settled in writing (Q3), and the realized returns it rests on are recomputed on a licensed feed or the Yahoo provenance is carried verbatim (Q5). Until then it stays off the site and out of git (#4). On GitHub, a committed `head1_scorecard.md` *is* a public page.

**Presentation rules, when it does appear** (each maps to a Marketing Rule / FTC principle):
1. **All companies, every scored filing** — never a subset, never sorted by hit rate by default (cherry-picking). Companies with no scorecard are listed with the reason ("no price history; disproportionately acquired or delisted names — 32 of 244"); their absence is outcome-correlated and the page says so.
2. **The universe block above any per-company table**: model hit rate with its Wilson interval; the `numeric_only` and `size_only` comparators on the same rows (if `size_only` matches or beats the model, that is the headline, not a footnote); the base rate (share of rows with positive excess); the sentence *"hit rates are descriptive — the inference is the pre-registered IC margin ladder"* verbatim.
3. **Methodology beside every number, not on another page**: call = tercile of the model's out-of-fold prediction within the test quarter (middle third "no call", excluded from the denominator — state the denominator); outcome = sign of the 63-session excess return versus the equal-weighted, self-excluded peer universe, window opening at the next session after the filing was public; rules frozen on the ratification date with the sha.
4. **Hypothetical-performance disclosures**: out-of-fold, backtested; no money was invested; no transaction costs, taxes, slippage or position sizing; **price return only, dividends not included** (the E2 run's Yahoo snapshot); labeler error rates and the train-overlap banner travel with the page; the walk-forward's per-quarter dispersion.
5. **Per-company rows only with the interval and the count**: a 7/10 company reads as [0.40, 0.89]; no per-company page exists without the universe block beside it.
6. **No forward statement.** "Would have said", never "will"; no "expected", no arithmetic converting a hit rate into money; no "accuracy" — "hit rate against a sign rule."
7. **Frozen and dated**, not live-updated; versioned artifact with its manifest sha; the paper log, if ever public, is a separate pre-registered artifact.
8. **Position disclosure** for any company the owner holds or has traded (#3).
9. **Not paired with a "try it on your company" box.** The scorecard for the fixed run universe is impersonal; a per-request scorecard for a visitor-named company is the personalization step (Q6).
10. **No "beats the market"**, no comparison to an index the run did not use (the ETF comparator is a P2 item on the licensed feed, and its own licence question).

## Q3 — EODHD Non-Professional "display" and the four artifacts

The clause forbids selling, reselling, retransmitting, redistributing, **displaying**, or granting access to "the Information" (the vendor's data), and grants storage, manipulation and analysis *"for private, non-commercial purposes"* to an individual acting *"solely in a personal capacity for their own personal investment activities."* No derived-works carve-out is quoted; I take the terms as the main session read them on 2026-09-11.

- **(a) raw price series** — "display" on its face. Forbidden on any public page, in git, in a video frame. No argument.
- **(b) a chart** — a chart is the Information rendered graphically; every market-data licence I know treats it as display, and this one has no carve-out to rely on. Forbidden publicly. (A chart in the owner's own private report is "analysis" and fine.)
- **(c) a derived quarterly excess return on a scorecard** — a single number per filing from which the series cannot be recovered (the peer benchmark is not shown) is a derived work, not "the Information"; the better reading is that it is not "display." The limb that bites is different: the analysis grant is for *private, non-commercial purposes*, and a public page is not private. Exposure is contractual — termination and a breach claim of modest size — not statutory, and it is untested language; a vendor can read "private" either way.
- **(d) an aggregate hit rate** — furthest derived (a count of sign matches over the universe); the "display" argument is weakest here and the "private purposes" question is the same as (c), with the lowest exposure of the four.

**What to do before any of (a)–(d) goes on a public page.**
1. Make the `PRICE_SOURCE_OPTIONS` §5 rule architectural now: no licensed price series or chart leaves the machine in any artifact, and the visitor path of any hosted box never touches the token (#6). That settles (a) and (b) permanently.
2. For (c) and (d): write to EODHD support describing the exact use — "a public, non-commercial page showing, per company and filing, a derived 63-session excess return versus a peer average and an aggregate hit rate; no prices, no chart, no series, no API access for visitors" — and ask for written confirmation under the Non-Professional plan or a quote for the tier that permits it. File the reply in `data/legal/`. If refused, the alternatives are a licence with a written derived-works carve-out (Sharadar's *"Attribution is not required for research outputs, backtest results, models, summary statistics … that do not display the Services Data itself"* is the only such text found — and its §6 still keeps display inside personal use, so ask them the same question) or a commercial tier.
3. Note that "others can use it" (a hosted tool for strangers) is outside "own personal investment activities" whatever is displayed; that is a licence-tier question, not a display question.
4. Attribution line if the vendor asks for one; keep every derived number with its methodology (Q2-3), which also makes the "not the Information" argument visibly true.

(ii) Rule 10b-5 and common-law negligent-misrepresentation exposure to that person for any misstatement; the MIT disclaimer covers the software, not advice conveyed through it. (iii) Every personal-tier data licence is breached if the other person used the tool or the account. (iv) If the owner holds the position, the scalping optics of #3 are live. Practical rule for that day: hand them the code and the reading aid, never the calls; never share the account; give them the disclosure block; do not customise anything for them.

## Q5 — The stored Yahoo snapshot

Facts: fetched 2026-08-24 from `query1.finance.yahoo.com` (`robots.txt`: `Disallow: /`; no published terms for the endpoint), 244 tickers, one-time, cached, `source` column preserved, parquet git-excluded, no new fetch (rulings §2 decision 8), disclosed in README:104 and `PRICES_NOTES.md`.

**Private research run.** Low exposure. Post-*Van Buren* (2021) and *hiQ v. LinkedIn* (9th Cir. 2022), accessing publicly available, unauthenticated data is unlikely to be "without authorization" under the CFAA, and `robots.txt` is not a contract. Browsewrap terms a script never saw are weakly enforceable; a few hundred requests cause no cognisable harm for trespass; prices are facts with no copyright (*Feist*). Residual: Yahoo's terms do prohibit scraping, so a breach theory exists on paper with no damages behind it. The disclosure already made is the right posture.

**Quoting results derived from it in a public write-up or on the site.** Legal exposure stays low — a rank correlation or a hit rate is a derived fact, not a copy — but three things are real: (1) the write-up becomes a public admission of scraping against a `Disallow: /` on a page you control, which is reputational and sits badly beside any "nothing scraped" line (#5); (2) every public scorecard number then traces to a source the README itself calls non-compliant, which a reviewer, employer or opposing attorney will read; (3) a cease-and-desist is possible and trivially complied with, but it would land on the product, not the research. What I would tell the owner: the research write-up may quote derived statistics with the provenance sentence verbatim, as README does now; never publish the parquet, a series, or a chart from it; **recompute any product-facing realized return on the licensed feed before it is public**, so the public scorecard traces to a licensed source (and then Q3 governs).

## Q6 — Which of the three flips the analysis, and why

- **A waitlist** does not flip it. It is a contact list; its obligations are privacy and CAN-SPAM (#8). It is, however, *evidence* — of "in the business" (building a user base) and of intent to distribute — that an attorney will weigh, and if its copy promises "get investment calls" it is holding out (#1).
- **A hosted "name a company, watch it read" box** flips it *only if* what comes back is a call. Returning the reading aid — the same labels-beside-passages report for anyone who types the same ticker, no price, no rank, no call — is an information service that behaves like a publication: impersonal, the same for everyone. Returning "outperform peers" for the security a visitor names is specific advice about a specific security on request; interactivity plus specificity is what *Lowe*'s "impersonal" prong and the staff's "specific advice" factor turn on, and with any compensation it is the adviser fact pattern.
- **A ranked list** flips it the most. A list of securities ordered by a model's view of their prospects is "the advisability of investing in" in its purest form; it is what a newsletter is. An impersonal, regularly published list can still be a publication under *Lowe*, but a continuously re-ranked algorithmic list on a website, paired with a track record, is not a periodical, several states regulate it more strictly, and it concentrates the scalping risk (#3) on the top of the list. It is the artifact that converts research into a recommendation product.
